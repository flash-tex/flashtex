\chapter{Kinds of Error}
\label{ch:kinds}

Every measurement mixes three ingredients: random scatter, systematic bias,
and mistakes. Random scatter averages away with repetition; bias does not,
and it is the subject of Section~\ref{sec:propagate} on
page~\pageref{sec:propagate}. Mistakes are handled by discipline, not
statistics.

Consider weighing a beaker on a balance that reads to $0.01$\,g. Repeated
readings might give $52.31$, $52.33$, $52.30$, $52.32$\,g. The scatter of
about $0.01$\,g is random; a calibration offset of $+0.05$\,g would be a
bias affecting every reading equally. Reporting $52.32 \pm 0.01$\,g states
the first and says nothing about the second.

\section{Propagation}
\label{sec:propagate}

When a result $f$ depends on measured inputs $x$ and $y$ with small
independent errors $\sigma_x$ and $\sigma_y$, the errors combine in
quadrature. For a sum this reduces to adding absolute variances; for a
product, relative variances add instead.
